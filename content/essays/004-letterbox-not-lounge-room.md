I spent June and July teaching people how to run their inbox, while quietly not running mine.

My good friend Daniel Sih and his family have been walking the Camino, and before he left he asked if I'd backfill him on his Productivity Masterclass series.

It's work I hadn't done hands-on in several years.

Four cohorts, six people in each, all of it over Zoom with people spread right across Australia.

The first thing that got me was the maths. My first Email Ninja session was in 2014. Twelve years of using these practices, and probably thousands of people I've taught them to since.

The second thing was worse.

One of the core disciplines is processing your inbox in blocks rather than grazing on it all day.

The inbox is where things arrive. It's not where you live. You choose when to open the digital envelopes.

In a group of six, someone pushed back on moving mail out of the inbox at all. They said:

{% quote %}I just need everything in front of me in the inbox. I'm worried that if I move it into a working folder it will go there to die.{% endquote %}

I remember nodding along and thinking, *fair enough, that's a common one.*

Then I thought, *hang on. Is my Outlook open on my second screen right now?*

It was.

They weren't describing a filing problem. They were describing which room in the house they trust.

Because there are only two ways to treat an inbox. It's a letterbox, or it's a lounge room.

A letterbox is somewhere things arrive and you go and check it. A lounge room is somewhere you sit, with the door open, waiting to see who wanders past.

The notifications, the second screen, the reply you fired off in a minute flat because it felt urgent. That's all just furniture you've moved in.

And that fear about the folder is exactly what keeps you in the lounge room. If you don't trust anywhere else in the house, of course you're going to sit where the mail lands.

I want to be fair to them, though. They're not wrong. Working folders absolutely do become graveyards if there's no rhythm of going back to them. A folder without a review is just a slower way of losing something.

The fear is well founded. It's the conclusion they've drawn from it that costs them.

And there are seasons where living in the inbox genuinely is the job. A launch. A crisis. A new role where you don't yet know what matters. I won't pretend otherwise.

But if I'm honest, that hasn't been my excuse. I'd just drifted.

Too many times recently I've wandered off on an email treasure hunt because something arrived that could have waited a day, a week, a month, or didn't need doing at all.

There's something about the immediacy of email that makes you feel you need to act faster than you really do. The cost is your big rocks. The important but not urgent work that actually matters.

So the Outlook window is minimised again. Calendar view only. A handful of blocks a day, rather than letting the postal service interrupt me every time an envelope drops.

And I'm calming my farm on urgency. Most of the speed I put on my replies comes from my own expectations, not from the people I'm replying to.

Somewhere in all of this, Daniel was walking across Spain. One foot, then the other, in order, with nothing else on offer.

He handed his masterclass over and went for a very long walk. I stayed home and taught it with the letterbox open beside me.

Here's the thing about teaching something for twelve years. It doesn't make you better at it. It just gives you a very well-lit room to notice you've stopped doing it in.

So, where in your week are you sitting in the lounge room, when you meant to be checking the letterbox?
