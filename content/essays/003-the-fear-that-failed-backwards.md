I spent the night before a client session worrying about my slides.

The slides were not the problem.

But I didn't know that yet, so I did what I always do when I'm nervous — over-prepared the framework, checked the timings, made sure every block of the day was filled with something valuable.

Back in July I sat down with Jack, Andy and Sophie — a leadership team of three — to work through their team health results against Patrick Lencioni's Five Dysfunctions of a Team.

They'd each done the pre-work assessment, so we had a report in front of us rather than a blank page.

The report told a good story straight away. Trust came in at 4.33 on a five-point scale.

Anything from 4 up counts as high, so that's a genuine strength, and it's not unusual for three people who've been in the trenches together.

The drop-off was on the layer directly above it. Conflict, 3.75. Accountability, 3.86. Their two lowest scores, sitting right on top of a strong foundation.

Which tracks with what they told me.

They weren't getting into ideological conflict — the healthy kind, the argument about the idea rather than the person. And they weren't sure how or when to hold each other to what they'd said they'd do.

About an hour in, the session went sideways.

We got onto their position titles, which had shifted around a fair bit over the previous couple of years, and I could see something wasn't sitting right with Andy.

Then he said it: *my general manager title makes me feel like a two dollar shop manager.*

That was nowhere in my plan. I had slides. I had a sequence.

I binned it and followed the tangent instead.

It turned out to be the most useful thing we did all morning, because it wasn't a discussion about titles.

It was a live rehearsal of the exact thing their report said they couldn't do — disagree about something that mattered, in the room, in front of each other, without it going sour.

Later we came back to the framework properly and did the conflict work.

What should disagreement actually look like here, in the room, in the moment? What currently stops one of us raising an unpopular opinion?

They landed on staying curious, giving each other time to explain a view, and being clear about what's genuinely negotiable in your own idea. They agreed to trial a phrase that signals a debate is starting rather than a fight — *can we stress test that?*

Then we went at accountability, and that's where the day turned.

Everyone answered one question on their own first. *Something I think I could do better as a teammate is…*

Then we opened it up: does anyone want to add to that, confirm it, or offer something else?

What came out wasn't a list of skills gaps. It was fears.

Jack went first. A fear of coming across as overpowering. Of not seeing his own blind spots. Of being ignorant. Of catastrophising. Of threatening the friendships.

And he could draw the line straight from those fears to his behaviour — it's why he'd been stopping short of asking the team whether they'd done the things they said they'd do.

Sophie answered almost straight away.

{% quote %}Jack, I really need to know more about what you're thinking. I want you to speak up more, because sometimes I feel like I'm not doing a good enough job.{% endquote %}

Jack laughed. It was relief, mostly. Like someone had just handed him permission.

That's the bit that stuck with me.

Because the permission was already there. It had been there the whole time. Nobody had asked, so nobody knew.

Look at the shape of what had just happened. Jack's fear was that speaking up would damage the relationship. Sophie's experience was that his silence had her doubting whether she was doing a good enough job.

His restraint wasn't protecting her. It was producing the exact harm he was trying to avoid.

The fear didn't just fail its test. It failed backwards.

And that's what an unsaid fear does. It never gets checked, so it gets treated as true, and it quietly sets your behaviour for years. Long enough that it stops looking like fear and starts looking like personality.

Jack isn't a leader who avoids accountability conversations. Jack is a leader carrying an untested belief about what happens if he has one.

Which brings me back to my slides.

I'd spent the night before that session preparing like a stranger who had to earn the right to push. But I'd been training with Jack at the gym at ridiculous o'clock for over a year. We'd spent days together at the Digital Health Festival.

I'd banked plenty of trust with him, then walked in and forgot to count it, and tried to substitute a framework for something I already had.

Trust works like capital. You accumulate it slowly, and you spend it on the things that are hard to do otherwise.

Like pushing a bloke on his job title an hour into a session. Or asking three friends to speak honestly into each other's blind spots.

But capital you've forgotten you have is capital you can't spend. Same as permission nobody's asked for. Same as a fear nobody's said out loud. All three sit there doing nothing, while everyone in the room behaves as though the opposite were true.

I've chosen not to run that second activity before. The only reason I was willing to that day is that the report told me the trust was there.

That night, Jack texted me: *Thanks so much for today, mate. Transformational session.*

I was chuffed. I was also a bit embarrassed at how surprised I was.

So here's what I'd sit with this week. Not the big question about trust. The small operational one.

Where are you waiting for a permission that's already been granted? And who would you have to ask to find out?
