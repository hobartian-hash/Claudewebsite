I was preparing a presentation for a board committee, and I caught myself doing the wrong job.

Since February I've been working with a Tasmanian social enterprise as they've implemented a new payroll, workforce management and HR system. 200+ staff. Five months of it.

That kind of project touches every person in an organisation, which is why the board committee wanted a proper read on where the risks sat.

I came in as the project management consultant. That was the contract.

But the actual purpose of the engagement was different. There was an up-and-coming project manager inside the business, someone who'd come across from IT, and the point of me being there was for them to come out the other side of this able to run the next one without me.

Which I knew. I'd said it out loud in the first week.

And yet there I was in July, opening a blank deck, working out which risks I thought the committee needed to see.

## Four steps, and only one thing changes

The model I keep coming back to for this sort of work is the apprenticeship square. I do, you watch. I do, you help. You do, I help. You do, I watch.

There are a few variations floating around, and I like this one because a nine-year-old could follow it. Four steps. The only thing that changes is who's holding the pen and who's standing behind them.

Nobody pulled me up on it. There was no conversation, no awkward moment, no one saying *shouldn't I be doing that?*

I just noticed it myself, sitting there with the deck open. Which is the uncomfortable bit, because it means it could just as easily have gone unnoticed.

So I closed the deck. And instead asked them to tell me what the risks were, and what they thought the committee needed to see.

What came back was better than anything I'd have put together on my own.

They went to the payroll SME. Payroll was the single biggest risk area on the whole project, the thing most likely to go wrong in a way 200+ people would feel on a Thursday afternoon. And what the SME told them was this:

{% quote %}I have no current issues with the system.{% endquote %}

If I'm honest, I wasn't feeling that confident myself. Which is exactly the point.

That line went in front of the committee carrying far more weight than my read on it ever could have, because it came from the person actually doing the work. And I'd never have got it, because I wouldn't have thought to go and ask.

## The thing I've had to sit with since

Holding on tight doesn't make you a bad consultant. It's frequently the reason you were engaged.

Someone was underwater, and you were the person who could take the hardest thing off their plate and make it disappear. That work is visible. It's billable. It gets thanked in the meeting.

Building the capability of the person beside you is none of those things. It's slower and it looks less impressive in the room. It's also genuinely worse in the short term, because their first version of the risk profile is going to be rougher than mine, and everyone in that committee room would rather have mine.

Except, as it turns out, it wasn't rougher. It was better.

Doing the work is what you get paid for. Building the person who does the work after you leave is the actual job.

And the stakes are what pull you backwards. Not laziness, not ego. Stakes. The higher the visibility of the moment, the more reasonable it feels to quietly take the pen back, right at the moment they most need to be the one holding it.

Which is why nobody ever tells you you've drifted back a step. The drift always looks like diligence.

If you're leading someone at the moment, or coaching someone, or handing something over, where are you on the square? And more usefully: where would *they* say you are?
