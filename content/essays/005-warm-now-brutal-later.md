“You just wanted that other person the whole time, didn't you?”

That's the last thing Greg ever said to me that mattered. He was right, which is the part that took me the better part of a decade to admit.

Greg isn't his name. I've changed it, partly because he doesn't need to be identified in something I'm publishing, and partly because in this story he's not the one who comes out badly.

Fifteen years ago I ran a health application support team. Greg came across with me from the implementation project, where he'd been a project officer, and rolled into a temporary role in the support team. We'd worked together for two years on the project, and then another six months once he moved into the temporary support role.

By then we weren't just colleagues. I'd met his family. We'd been camping together. We'd shared a bit of life outside of work, the kind of sharing that doesn't happen accidentally and doesn't happen with most people you work with.

I also knew things about Greg's work that I never said to him.

He was excellent with people. Genuinely good. Everyone liked dealing with him, and in a support team that matters more than people give it credit for. But the technical side wasn't there. And when something needed to be pulled apart, analysed and answered clearly, he struggled.

I didn't have to go looking for evidence of this. It came to me.

More than once, after a meeting, a stakeholder would find me quietly and ask whether Greg's training guide could be made a bit more professional before it went out. Whether the document could be made easier to follow. Nobody was being cruel about it. They just needed it to be right, and they'd worked out that the way to get it right was to have a word with me.

So here's what I did with that.

I gave the work to someone else. Someone genuinely gifted at writing and styling documents, which is a thing I'm good at spotting in people, and one of the few parts of this I'd still do the same way today.

Then I had to explain the arrangement to Greg. I told him this person was going to look after that side of things from now on. Which was true. It was also a way of moving the work without ever telling him why it was moving.

That's the moment I'd go back to if I could pick one. Not the interview. Not the accusation afterwards. That moment, where a true explanation was quietly doing the job of a false one.

I said none of it. Not properly. Not in a form he could have used. And the tidier the arrangement got, the less chance there was of him ever finding out he had a problem.

Then the permanent role was advertised. Greg applied, along with several others. I was one of the hiring managers on the panel.

He didn't get it. Someone else did.

He was cut to pieces by it, and he hadn't seen it coming, which in hindsight is completely reasonable. He'd been doing the job for six months, after two years alongside me on the project. Someone he trusted was on the panel. Why would he see it coming?

So he asked me the question, and it wasn't really a question.

It ended the friendship. Working and personal, more or less overnight.

## For years, I had a version of this where I was fine

The version went like this. It's a merit-based process. Relationships don't get you the job. Greg was unrealistic about his chances and that isn't my problem to solve. He's an adult. He should have known.

I believed that for a long time.

I was wrong.

## The mechanism

I only have proper language for this because I've since done the CliftonStrengths 34, which ranks all thirty-four themes rather than just handing you your top five. The bottom of the list turns out to be the more useful half.

My top five are Maximizer, Developer, Individualization, Relator and Includer. Every one of them is about people. Seeing potential in them, understanding what makes them tick, building something close and durable, making sure nobody's left outside the circle.

My bottom five are Command, Input, Deliberative, Restorative and Competition.

| Top five, what I reach for | Bottom five, what I avoid |
|---|---|
| **Maximizer** Turns good into excellent | **Command** Says the hard thing out loud |
| **Developer** Sees what people could become | **Input** Gathers and keeps the evidence |
| **Individualization** Reads what makes each person tick | **Deliberative** Weighs the risk before deciding |
| **Relator** Goes deep with a few | **Restorative** Runs at what is broken |
| **Includer** Makes sure nobody is left out | **Competition** Measures against everyone else |

*Four of the five on the left are relationship themes. None of the five on the right are. Theme names are Gallup's, the one-line descriptions are mine.*

Now look at what that combination does under pressure.

The situation with Greg needed Command, which is simply the willingness to say the unpopular thing in the room and hold it. It needed Deliberative, which is weighing a decision properly and naming the risk out loud well before it arrives. It needed Input, which would have meant actually gathering and documenting the evidence rather than carrying a vague, uncomfortable sense of it around for two and a half years.

I had none of those. Not a scrap.

So I did what I'm good at instead. I built the relationship. I encouraged him. I found the potential in him and told him about it, because that's what I do without having to try. And when the evidence turned up on my desk, I used Maximizer on it. I found the person who could make the documents excellent, handed it over, and made the problem disappear.

Which is the part that took me longest to understand. Moving that work wasn't me being weak in the moment. It was me being genuinely good at something. I solved for the quality of the training guide, which is a real problem and I solved it well. I just didn't solve for Greg.

Warm now, brutal later.

That's the trade I made, and I never once knew I was making it. Every conversation I softened felt like kindness in the moment. It bought me a comfortable afternoon. It also quietly pushed the cost of the truth further down the track. And when that day arrived, the bill didn't come to me. It came to Greg, on the worst possible morning, from someone he trusted.

This is the thing about operating in your weaknesses. It rarely feels like failure at the time. It feels like doing what you're good at. That's exactly why it's hard to catch.

## In fairness to my younger self

I was fairly junior at managing people. Nobody had taught me how to have that conversation, and I'm not sure I'd have recognised it as a conversation that needed having. The relationship was genuinely good, which made every individual decision to say nothing feel warm rather than gutless.

It wasn't that I was dodging it and knew it. I didn't have the awareness to know what I was dodging.

I also think there's something in this that isn't just about me. If you lead with relationship strengths, the workplace tends to reward you for it early and reward you for it loudly. You're the one people open up to. You're good in the room. Nobody sits you down and says, by the way, the exact thing that makes you good at this will eventually cost someone something. You have to find that out on your own, usually the way I found it out.

## But if I'm honest

The hardest part of this isn't that Greg was hurt.

It's that he was right.

I did want the other person. I'd seen what they brought and I knew, well before the panel ever sat down. He read the situation correctly. The only thing he had wrong was the timing, and the timing was my doing, because I'd let him keep believing something I'd quietly stopped believing myself.

That's the sentence I've had to sit with. Not “he was unrealistic.” *I let him keep believing it.*

## What I'd do now

Fifteen years on, I'd catch it earlier.

Not because I've developed a Command strength. I haven't, and at this point I'd say I won't. That's not really how it works.

I'd catch it because I've learnt to recognise a particular feeling. It's a low hum of *this isn't going well and I'm not at my best.* Back then I read that feeling as a signal to try harder, be more encouraging, lean further into the relationship. Now I read it as a signal to go and get someone.

That's the actual antidote, and it's less noble than it sounds. It isn't about developing your weaknesses. It's about saying out loud that you're not the person for this particular bit, and asking someone who is. A mentor. A peer. Anyone with the strengths sitting where your gaps are.

## So what actually stops us?

Here's the part I find most interesting, because it isn't a knowledge problem.

Most of the time we already know exactly who to ask.

I could have named three people who'd have walked into that conversation with Greg without breaking a sweat. Straight in, kind but clear, out the other side, everyone still standing. I knew who they were at the time. I asked none of them.

So it isn't ignorance. What is it?

I don't think it's pride, mostly. Pride is the flattering explanation, the one that at least makes you sound like a strong character with a tragic flaw. Ego's in there somewhere, but it's not the engine either.

I think it's simpler and smaller than that. Asking makes the thing real.

The moment you say out loud, “I need help having a hard conversation with Greg,” you've admitted there's a hard conversation to be had. It exists now. Someone else knows it exists. And now you have to have it. Right up until you ask, you get to keep the possibility that it might sort itself out, that he'll improve, that someone else will handle it, that the situation will change and the conversation will never be needed.

It never sorts itself out. But not asking lets you keep believing it might, and that belief is worth a lot in the short term.

So we don't ask. And the not asking is the same trade all over again, just smaller.

## The question

Think of a situation you're in right now that isn't going well. One where you already know, if you're honest, that you're operating outside what you're actually good at.

Now think of the person you'd ask for help. You almost certainly already know who they are. You probably thought of them before you finished reading the last paragraph.

What's the reason you've given yourself for not asking them yet?

And is it a real reason, or is it just warm now?
