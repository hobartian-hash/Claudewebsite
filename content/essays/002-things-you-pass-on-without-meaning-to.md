Kate asked me what my dad was like.

We were at Mission Beach in June, five days into a trip I'd booked with Fathering Adventures, the program Darren and Mel run for fathers and their kids.

I did the same trip with Isla last year, when she was fourteen. This time it was Kate, now eighteen, and fifteen other dad and daughter pairs.

I told her I was always in awe of his intelligence. His vocabulary. The way he could use language to make a point land.

Then I told her about Sale of the Century. Dad would sit there and answer the questions in the same beat as the contestants on the show. Not after them. With them.

I used to take the piss out of him for it mercilessly.

And I told her the truth, which is that I secretly admired it.

If I'm honest, I was nervous about this one in a way I hadn't been with Isla. Kate's eighteen. She's got her own life.

My fear going in was that she didn't feel as close to me as I thought she did, or as I was hoping she did.

It turned out to be the opposite.

Somewhere in the middle of it — between snorkelling the reef and rafting the Tully — I found myself explaining to my daughter what her grandfather had imprinted on me. The good parts. A sense of humour. A way of using language to make a point.

And the parts I'd rather not have. Reaching for humour when I'm uncomfortable. Over-explaining something that would land better said simply.

Here's what I only noticed afterwards.

The story I told Kate about my dad was itself a demonstration of the thing he passed down. I didn't tell her he was brilliant. I told her I took the piss out of him. I used the joke to get near the admiration without having to say it out loud.

Forty-odd years on, sitting on a beach with my own daughter, doing the exact same thing.

That's what an imprint is. It transfers whether anyone intends it or not. Nobody chose it. Nobody said it out loud. It just arrived, and it's still arriving, generations down.

A blessing is the other thing entirely.

On the last part of the trip, I spoke words over Kate that Caz and I had chosen together beforehand. We told her we had three expectations of her.

To pursue a relationship with God that feels real to her. Not borrowed, not inherited, but genuinely hers.

To be brave. To trust herself, question and doubt herself less, and not be afraid of making mistakes, because that's how courage and character get built.

And that life will always be serious, but to remember to also find the joy. To never stop bringing lightness and laughter to the people around her, even under the weight of it all.

Nothing in that is accidental. Caz and I sat down and decided it. Then I said it to her face, out loud, in front of people.

That's the whole difference.

{% quote %}Imprint is what you pass on anyway. A blessing is what you pass on on purpose.{% endquote %}

Most cultures throughout history have had a formal moment for this. A deliberate calling of a young person into adulthood by the people who raised them, often marked with a new name.

In the West, we've largely lost it. We've got an eighteenth birthday, a driver's licence, maybe a graduation.

And I don't think we lost it because we stopped caring about our kids. We lost it because the imprint happens on its own. It requires nothing of you. So you never notice that the blessing didn't get said.

To be fair, plenty of good gets handed down accidentally. My dad never sat me down and appointed me his vocabulary or his humour. I got them anyway, and I'm grateful for both.

And I'd be uncomfortable suggesting you need a program in Far North Queensland to be a decent father.

This trip cost several thousand dollars by the time I'd added program fees and flights, plus a night in Townsville at a $300 hotel I'd charitably describe as the dodgiest I've stayed in. Not bitter about it at all. Twice in twelve months, once for each daughter.

Plenty of fathers do this properly without spending a cent. The money didn't buy the blessing. It bought five days where I couldn't get out of saying it.

But accidental transmission has no quality control. You hand on the vocabulary and the deflection in the same parcel. Whatever you haven't looked at, they get.

Which is why the third expectation is the one I keep coming back to.

We told Kate to find the joy. To keep bringing lightness and laughter to people, even when things are heavy.

That's my dad's imprint. That's the humour I inherited from a man answering Sale of the Century questions in the same beat as the contestants.

Same inheritance — but this time somebody looked at it first, took out the part where it's used to dodge things, and handed on the good half deliberately.

That's the bit you can only do on purpose.

So here's the question I'd sit with, and I don't think you need a trip to Mission Beach to answer it.

Your kids are already receiving the imprint. That's happening regardless.

What have you actually said out loud?
